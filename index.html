01
Hormuz Headlines Hit the Futures Screen
NYMEX traders react to geopolitical risk by pricing worst-case scenarios into futures contracts. This creates an immediate spike in ULSD front-month contracts — often 10–20% within 48 hours — before any single barrel of supply is actually affected. Fleet managers see pump prices jump because rack prices follow NYMEX within days. This is the noise.
02
The Real Picture Lives in EIA Inventory Data
Released every Wednesday, the EIA WPSR tells you whether the U.S. physically has enough distillate to absorb the shock. Right now, stocks are 11% below the 5-year seasonal average — meaning the market was already structurally tight before Hormuz. That's what sustains elevated prices after the speculative premium fades.
03
Separate the Premium from the Floor
The analyst's job: identify the inventory-implied floor (what prices should be based on physical supply) vs. the speculative premium (fear priced in by futures traders). In normal disruptions, speculative premiums fade within weeks once the immediate threat passes. Sustained closures — like Hormuz — break that pattern. The floor persists as long as the physical deficit persists, regardless of what headlines do next.
